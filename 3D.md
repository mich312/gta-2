# 3D — the simulation gets a third axis

`SHIP.md` §12 declined this, and was overruled. The decline is worth keeping
on the record because its reasoning is now the work plan: the simulation is 2D
throughout — `collide.ts`, `roadgrid.ts`, oriented-box vehicle collision,
worldgen, traffic routing — and giving it a z axis rewrites roughly forty per
cent of `shared/`.

This document is how that is done without losing the thing the rewrite is
most likely to cost: determinism.

---

## 0. The constraint that shapes everything

**No physics engine.**

Rapier, Jolt, Ammo and Cannon are all excluded, and not on taste. None
guarantees bit-identical results across platforms and versions, and this
repository has four separate systems that require exactly that:

| System | What breaks without bit-identical `step()` |
|---|---|
| Replays | Stop re-simulating to identical hashes. The desync alarm goes off permanently, so it stops being an alarm. |
| Bot harness | Fails the build on hash divergence. It would fail always. |
| `pnpm parity` | Node and the browser stop agreeing, and the offline host becomes a fork of the game rather than the game. |
| Prediction | Rewind/replay reconciliation replays inputs on the client and expects the server's answer. Non-determinism becomes permanent rubber-banding. |

So 3D collision is *written*, the way the 2D collision was written: exact ops,
axis-separated, sub-stepped, no square roots in the resolution path. That is
slower to build than `import RAPIER` and it is the only version that keeps the
repository's best property.

Everything in `ROADMAP.md` §0 continues to hold. Two invariants do most of the
work here:

- **`step()` stays bit-identical everywhere.** New subsystems get a fixed
  position in the step order and iterate in sorted-id order.
- **`state.rng` advances only inside `step()`, in a fixed sequence.** The
  volume grid draws no random numbers at all — it is a pure function of the
  map — precisely so this conversion does not shift every downstream draw.

**Replays from before the conversion will not re-simulate.** That is
unavoidable: entity state gains fields and the step order changes. It must be
called out in `PROGRESS.md` when it lands, and the existing replay corpus
should be re-recorded against the first stable 3D build so the oracle exists
again on the other side.

## 1. The world model

A tile is no longer solid or not. Every tile carries a short stack of
**spans** — half-open `[bottom, top)` intervals of solid matter. You stand on
a span's top; you are blocked when your own vertical extent overlaps one.

```
road tile      [EARTH, 0)                          ground at 0
building       [EARTH, storeys × 24)               wall at street, roof on top
bridge         [EARTH, -8)  +  [40, 46)            water below, deck above
open water     [EARTH, -8)                         nothing to stand on at 0
```

The bridge line is the whole argument for the change. Today `T_BRIDGE` is one
tile that means "solid to boats, open to cars" — a special case that expresses
exactly one situation and collapses the moment a second one appears. As two
spans it is not a special case at all, and a boat passing under a car falls out
of the same query that stops you walking into a wall.

Stored flat: `offset`/`count` per tile indexing shared `bottoms`/`tops`
arrays. 57,600 tiles at 240×240, almost all with one span.

**Status: built.** `shared/src/world/volume.ts`,
`shared/src/world/collide3.ts`, 17 tests in `shared/test/volume.test.ts`
including a boat sailing under a bridge while a car drives over the same tile,
step-up onto a kerb, refusing to step up where there is no headroom, falling
off a bridge into the river, and no tunnelling at 400 px a tick.

# Wave X — the world has volume

## X1 — Spans, and collision against them (M) — **built**

Above. The load-bearing risk was whether deterministic 3D collision was
writable at all; it is, and it is about 400 lines.

## X2 — The volume grid replaces the tile grid at every call site (M)

`isSolidTile`, `isSolidAtWorld`, `moveWithCollision` and `boxInSolid` have
callers throughout `sim/`. Each has to move to the volume equivalent and pass
a height. Mechanical but broad, and it is where the old model's assumptions
are actually written down — every caller that assumed "solid means solid at
all heights" is a decision to make.

Keep both paths alive behind a flag until the bot harness and `pnpm parity`
are green on the 3D one.

## X3 — Oriented boxes get a third dimension (M)

Vehicles collide as oriented boxes, not AABBs (`vehicle.ts`). The z extent is
separable from the rotation — a car is an OBB in xy and an interval in z — so
this is a smaller change than it sounds, and the separation is worth
preserving rather than moving to full 3D OBB tests.

---

# Wave Y — everything that moves has a height

## Y1 — Entities gain z, vz and a standing height (M)

Players and vehicles already carry `z`/`vz`, for stunt jumps and aircraft
altitude — but nothing collides with them vertically. Pedestrians, projectiles
and props have no z at all.

This is the item that **changes the wire**. `z` per entity on the snapshot,
`PROTOCOL_VERSION` bumped, and the bandwidth accounted against the 50 KB/s
gate. Budget: roughly 2 bytes per entity at the existing quantisation, against
~10.5 KB/s measured today. It fits, but it must be measured, not assumed.

## Y2 — Gravity for everything, not just stunts (M)

One integrator, applied to every body, replacing the special cases in
`stepStunts` and the aircraft path. Falls out of `move3` — the work is
deleting the special cases without changing how a stunt jump feels.

## Y3 — Projectiles in three dimensions (M)

Ballistic arcs, shots that pass over a low wall, and a rocket that hits a
building's face rather than its footprint. Also where "shoot someone on a
roof" becomes possible, which is a design question as much as a technical one.

---

# Wave Z — navigation in three dimensions

The hardest wave, and the one most likely to be underestimated.

## Z1 — The road graph gets levels (L)

`roadgrid.ts` assumes one road surface per tile. A bridge over a road is two,
and they are not connected. The graph becomes nodes at (tile, level) with
explicit vertical transitions — ramps, and eventually stairs and lifts.

## Z2 — Traffic and police drive it (L)

`traffic.ts` is 1,301 lines and `police.ts` is 1,095, both routing over the
flat grid. Pursuit that can lose you by taking the wrong level is a genuinely
better chase and a genuinely harder AI.

## Z3 — Pedestrians on multiple levels (M)

Pavements, footbridges, and the rule that stops a crowd walking off a roof.

# Wave W3 — the renderer

## W3a — The city as geometry, and the game in it (M) — **spiked, and live**

`client/src/three/cityView.ts` and `/city3d.html`. Three.js, orthographic
camera with a pitch, geometry built **from the volume grid** — so the thing
the collision resolves against and the thing you look at are the same two
numbers.

Measured on a box with no GPU, which is why the frame rate is not quoted:
**9 draw calls, 57,767 instances, 762k triangles** for the whole 240×240 city.
Draw count is a property of how the scene is built and is the same on any
machine; the software renderer's frame rate is not. `evidence/city-3d.png`.

One obvious optimisation already visible: every tile is its own box, so
instance count tracks tile count. Greedy meshing across runs of equal-height
ground would cut it by a large factor. Not needed at 9 draws, but it is the
first thing to reach for if it ever is.

**It is now playable.** `/city3d.html` runs the offline host in a worker and
feeds the same `Interpolator` the 2D renderer uses, so both views draw the
identical interpolated state and a disagreement between them is a rendering
bug rather than two games. WASD drives, E gets in and out, Space fires.
Pedestrians, police, vehicles and other players are instanced bodies placed
per frame. `evidence/city-3d-live.png`.

**The Chinatown Wars look, concretely** (`client/src/three/toon.ts`). Banded
toon shading — a nearest-filtered gradient map quantising diffuse light to
three tones — plus black outlines by inverted hull: the mesh drawn again,
fattened along its normals in view space, back faces only. Both are also the
cheap options, which is why the art direction suits a small team. The hull
beats a depth/normal edge-detect pass here because it works with
`InstancedMesh`, needs no render target, and the interior creases it misses
are the ones the banding already draws.

**Two bugs worth recording, because neither was visible until it was fixed.**
Building spans run `[EARTH, top]`, and clamping the drawn bottom to
`top - 64` — intended to stop the earth wasting the depth buffer — capped
every building at the same height whatever its storeys said. A twelve-storey
tower drew exactly as tall as a bungalow, which is the entire point of having
heights. Clamp to a fixed floor instead. And every building took one hardcoded
palette variant, so the city was monotone grey; buildings now bucket by the
same per-building hash `TileLayer.roofColor` and `ExtrudeLayer` use, so a
block is the colour in 3D that it is in 2D.

**The camera is perspective, looking straight down — and the first version
was wrong.** It shipped orthographic, with a comment arguing for it: that a
perspective camera "would make the same building look different depending on
where it sat on screen". That is true, and it is the *point*. Under ortho you
only ever see roofs and the city reads as a floorplan. Under perspective a
building away from the centre splays outward and shows the face turned toward
the camera — the north side of buildings north of you, the south side of those
south of you. That splay is the original GTA look, and it is the same effect
`extrude.ts` fakes by hand in 2D for exactly this reason. `FOV_Y` is the whole
dial: narrow flattens to a floorplan, wide lies the corners down.

A consequence worth knowing before it reads as a bug: a building *below* the
screen centre shows its **north** wall **above** its roof, because perspective
pushes its top further from centre than its base.

**Facades are computed in the shader** (`facade.ts`), from world position, so
one material serves every building height. Storey lines fall on world Z,
window columns on world X or Y depending on which way the wall faces, and the
ground floor gets a shopfront instead of a window grid. The alternatives both
defeat the instancing that makes the city cheap: geometry means a box per
window, and textures mean a different UV scale — so a different material —
per height. Injected with `onBeforeCompile` into `MeshToonMaterial` so the
banding, shadows and lighting keep working.

**Every body is the 2D sprite, extruded** (`spriteMesh.ts`). This is W3c, and
it turned out to be the cheap route rather than the expensive one.

`shared/data/sprites.json` was never really a description of pixels. Every
shape in it carries a `z` — a surface height — and the sprite generator
rasterises the shapes and then relights each pixel from that height field,
which is what makes flat art read as solid. The file has always been a stack
of extrusions that happened to be flattened on the way out. The generator here
reads the same file and does not flatten it: `rect` becomes a box, `poly` an
extruded profile, `disc` and `ellipse` cylinders, `line` a box along its
segment, `mirrorY` a mirrored twin.

What that buys over hand-modelling:

- **One source of art.** A new vehicle is still a JSON edit and arrives in
  both renderers at once. Two pipelines for one game is how the 2D and 3D
  versions of a car drift until nobody can say which is right.
- **The paint jobs come free.** `variants.body` already emits ten colourways
  per vehicle. In 3D that is ten paint schemes for no extra authoring, keyed
  off vehicle id so a car keeps its colour. A car's ten variants are ten
  pools of one geometry — ten draws for every car in the city, against one
  draw per car if the colour were per-instance.
- **The proportions are already tuned.** Somebody made a 53×29 car read as a
  car. Re-deriving that by eye throws that work away.

The one liberty taken is `Z_EXAGGERATION = 1.5`: authored heights were tuned
to *imply* depth under a relighting pass, and read slightly squashed under a
camera that can actually see them. It is the smallest knob that fixes it
without touching the art.

`models.ts` survives only as the fallback for a kind the sheet has no entry
for — today that is `fire` alone. Without it a missing sprite would be an
invisible car rather than a plain one.

One bug worth recording: `ExtrudeGeometry` returns non-indexed geometry while
the box and cylinder primitives are indexed, and `mergeGeometries` requires
all or none. Every part is flattened with `toNonIndexed()` before merging —
and it has to happen before the vertex colours are written, because it changes
the vertex count.

### The dressing pass

Everything above got the shapes right and still looked like a diagram. Four
things fixed that, and all four are the same move: put back what the 2D
renderer already does, from the same data.

**Planting** (`scenery.ts`). Trees and bushes on park and woodland tiles, at
`hash2(tx, ty, 71) > 0.92` and `> 0.87` — the *identical* hash and thresholds
the 2D tile layer uses. That is not tidiness: it means a park has its trees in
the same places in both renderers, so switching views does not rearrange the
world and a landmark you navigate by stays put. Baked once into two instanced
meshes; ~8% of tiles carry something.

**Props from the simulation.** Lamps, bins, fences and barrels, placed per
frame from the interpolated snapshot because they can be destroyed — and a
broken one swaps to its `_broken` sprite, which the sheet already carries.

**Road markings.** A dashed centre line down each carriageway. The first
version drew a dash on every tile edge and turned the road network into a
chequerboard, because a road tile cannot tell from its own coordinates whether
it is the middle of a four-lane street. Fixed the way the 2D layer does it:
measure the contiguous run through each tile on both axes on the CPU — on a
horizontal street the *vertical* run is the carriageway width, so its midpoint
is the centre line — and bucket tiles into plain / centre-along-x /
centre-along-y. Three materials, three draws, markings in the same places as
2D. Junctions are left unmarked rather than crossing two lines through them.

**Ground grain.** Grass, pavement and lots get two scales of noise and an
optional darkened tile seam, which is the shader version of the speckle the 2D
tile layer paints. It matters more than it sounds: a flat fill over a whole
park reads as a placeholder, and the same colour with a few per cent of noise
in it reads as a surface.

Plus the frame itself: ACES filmic tone mapping instead of the linear default,
so sunlit roofs stop clipping to flat white and the cel bands stop reading as
posterisation; MSAA on; device pixel ratio honoured.

Cost of the dressing pass: 165 draws, 2.9M triangles.

### The detail pass

From a camera hanging straight down, **roofs are most of what you see of a
building**, and a flat coloured rectangle is where a city stops looking built.
That is the single biggest detail gap this view had, and three things closed
it.

**Parapets.** A lip around every roof, on the sides with a non-building
neighbour only — so a block is rimmed at its outline rather than gridded tile
by tile. Real geometry, ~6k instances in one draw. This does more for how a
building reads than anything else in the pass.

**Rooftop clutter.** Plant units, vents and hatches scattered on *interior*
roof tiles at `hash2(tx, ty, 61)` against the same thresholds the 2D roof
painter uses. Interior-only is what stops an air-conditioning unit hanging
over the street.

**Kerbs** (`KERB_Z` in `volume.ts`). Pavement stands 3 px above the
carriageway. Put in the volume model rather than the renderer because it is
real: it is inside every mover's step-up allowance so nobody is stopped by it,
and it is the surface a car mounting the pavement actually climbs. It is also
what makes a street read as a street instead of a coloured stripe.

**Crossings.** Zebra stripes on the road tiles approaching a junction.
Anchored to junctions — road wide on both axes — rather than to kerbs, because
every kerbside tile touches a pavement and a kerb test stripes the whole
length of every street instead of its mouth. The first version of this
function returned 0 on every path, which is worth recording: dead code that
typechecks and renders nothing looks exactly like a feature that is switched
off.

Cost of the detail pass on top: 179 draws, 3.2M triangles with the live
population.

**Why Three.js and not React Three Fiber.** R3F's value is a declarative scene
graph reconciled against React state. This codebase has no React, and its
state is a 30 Hz snapshot stream interpolated per frame in a hand-rolled rAF
loop — every entity transform changes every frame. R3F's own guidance for that
is `useFrame` plus direct ref mutation, which is imperative Three.js with a
reconciler attached. Babylon and PlayCanvas are engines where this wants a
library.

## W3b — Prediction and the HUD through the 3D path (L)

Entities landed with W3a. What has not: **client-side prediction** — the 3D
path interpolates the local player like any other, where `main.ts` predicts
and reconciles by rewind/replay. Against a worker host the round trip is
sub-millisecond so it does not read as lag, but over a real socket it would,
and the moment co-op exists it has to come back.

The existing renderer is 6,528 lines and does considerably more than draw
boxes: interpolation, prediction ghosts, the damage diagram, the minimap, the
kill feed, decals, skid marks. All of it has to arrive in the 3D path, and the
761-line Canvas lighting pass gets **deleted** rather than ported — real
lights and shadow maps do that job.

## W3c — Art (L, and the real cost)

57 procedurally-generated sprites become meshes.

The lucky part: `shared/data/sprites.json` already describes flat shapes —
`rect`, `disc`, `ellipse`, `poly`, `line` — **each carrying a `z` height**,
and the generator already relights every pixel from that height field. That is
an extrusion recipe that happens to be rasterised. The same JSON should be
able to emit meshes instead of pixels, which would make the existing art
pipeline a 3D pipeline without authoring anything new.

**Prove that early.** If it holds, the art cost of this conversion drops by an
order of magnitude, and it is a day's work to find out.

# Wave V — worldgen builds vertically

## V1 — Buildings with interiors (L)

Currently a shop interior is punched out of a footprint and open to the sky.
With volumes, a building can have floors, and "go inside" stops being a
doorway teleport.

## V2 — Multi-level road geography (M)

Flyovers, underpasses, multi-storey car parks, tunnels. The generator already
places bridges; this generalises that to any two surfaces at different heights.

## V3 — Heights become authored, not derived (S)

`buildingStoreys` is a hash of the footprint — good enough to prove the model,
wrong for a skyline anybody designed. This is the same pivot `SHIP.md` U1
makes for the map: the generator produces a starting point and a human
overrules it.

## 3. Sequencing

**X2 → X3 → Y1 → Y2** is the critical path, and nothing in Wave Z or W3b
should start before Y1 lands, because both consume entity z.

W3a and W3c can run in parallel with all of it — the viewer needs no sim
changes, and the sprite-to-mesh question is independent and worth answering
early because of what it does to the art budget.

**The gates, in order:**

1. `pnpm test` green with the 3D path on.
2. `pnpm bots` — 0 desyncs, tick spread 0, under the 50 KB/s gate with `z` on
   the wire.
3. `pnpm parity` — Node and browser identical, on the 3D sim.
4. Replay corpus re-recorded, and re-simulating to identical hashes.

Until 2, 3 and 4 are green together, the conversion is not done however good
it looks.

---

## 4. Risks, ranked

1. **Determinism erodes quietly.** Not in one commit — in twenty, each adding
   a `Math.hypot` or a normalise to the resolution path. Mitigation: the four
   gates above run in CI from X2's first commit, not at the end.
2. **Wave Z is underestimated.** Multi-level navigation is where city games
   spend years. Mitigation: keep the flat graph working, and let level
   transitions be explicit, sparse and authored rather than inferred.
3. **The wire budget.** Y1 puts z on every entity. Measured today at
   ~10.5 KB/s against a 50 KB/s gate, so there is room — but props, peds and
   projectiles all gaining a field at once is the kind of thing that is fine
   three times and not the fourth.
4. **The renderer rewrite outruns the sim.** A beautiful 3D view of a 2D
   simulation is a demo, not progress. Mitigation: W3b does not start until
   Y1 lands.
5. **Art becomes the schedule.** If sprites do not extrude cleanly to meshes,
   W3c is the long pole and everything else waits behind it. Mitigation: test
   it in week one, not month six.

---

## 5. What this document still declines

- **A physics engine.** §0. Not negotiable while replays, the bot harness,
  parity and prediction all depend on bit-identical stepping.
- **A perspective camera.** Orthographic keeps a building the same shape
  wherever it sits on screen, which is the readability this genre trades
  verticality for. A pitch angle gives height somewhere to go without giving
  that up.
- **Free-form 3D geometry.** The world stays a grid of columns. Arbitrary
  meshes would need a broadphase, a mesh collider and a content pipeline to
  author them, and would buy very little a city needs.
- **Aim-relative movement and a rotating camera**, still — `GTA.md` §10
  costed both and the reasons have not changed just because there is a z axis.
